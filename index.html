Upscaling Line Art Without Destroying the Lines

At 4×, interpolated upscaling loses 12% of the ink pixels in a line drawing. Then sharpening adds 20% back. The lost 12% is softness; the added 20% is the halo that makes a clean drawing look like it is glowing.

14 November 2026 · ink-coverage measurement on a synthetic line-art test

Line art breaks upscalers in a way photographs do not, and the reason is that line art has almost no mid-tones. A photo is a continuous field of values where an average is meaningful. A drawing is a binary decision — ink or paper — and every resampling filter is built to produce averages, which on a binary image means a grey fringe that belongs to neither.

The measurement

Test image: horizontal lines at 1, 2 and 3px width plus a 2px circle outline, black on white, 400×400. Downscale to 200×200 (2×), upscale back to 400×400 with each method, then measure ink coverage — the percentage of pixels below mid-grey. Ground truth is 1.851%.

Three things to read out of this:

1. The smooth filters eat lines. Bicubic and Lanczos both land around 1.71–1.72%, about 7% below the ground truth. A 1px line upscaled with a good interpolation kernel becomes a 1px line with two grey shoulders — and at the threshold where you count "ink," those shoulders fall on the wrong side. Thin lines get thinner, then disappear.

2. Nearest over-preserves. It lands at 1.978%, above the truth, because it replicates hard pixel blocks. That is not accuracy — it is a blocky line that is nominally the right thickness but visually wrong, with stair-stepping along every diagonal. If you have ever upscaled line art with nearest and wondered why the curves look like a staircase, this is the number behind it.

3. Sharpening overshoots hard. Every sharpened variant jumps to 2.18–2.24%, which is 18–21% above the ground truth. Unsharp mask works by subtracting a blurred copy, and on a line that produces a dark core with bright rails on either side. Those bright rails, and the enlarged dark core, both count as ink. The line is now thicker than it was drawn, with a halo.

Why this is worse for line art than for photos. On a photo, a halo lands on texture that partially masks it. On line art, the halo lands on flat white paper. There is nothing to hide it, so a sharpened drawing reads as slightly luminous — a characteristic "AI upscale" look that people recognise without being able to name.

A number that stays flat while the line disappears

Everything above counts ink with a single number: the percentage of pixels below mid-grey. That number is an average over every stroke in the frame, and an average over thin strokes is the wrong instrument. So I rebuilt the same test with two changes. The source is now anti-aliased by exact area coverage instead of being hard black-or-white, because that is what a real export looks like. And every result is measured twice: once as ink mass — total ink in full-black-pixel equivalents — and once as peak ink, the darkest value the line still reaches. Mass says how much ink is in the frame. Peak says whether the line is still a line.

The two disagree, and they disagree most where the advice matters. Scene: three bands of six horizontal lines at 1, 2 and 3px, 400×400, downscaled 2× and back up with each kernel pair.

The threshold metric saturates. Look at the column that reads <128: it is 0.00% for all sixteen kernel pairs. Every one of them reports that the 1px band contains no ink at all. On those same pixels, ink mass ranges from −0.4% to +28.6%. A metric that returns zero across a 29-point range of the quantity it is meant to measure is not imprecise — it is blind. The reason is numerical: a 1px black line, box-downscaled 2×, becomes two rows of exactly 128, and 128 is not below 128.

Mass is conserved while the object changes. Take box down / box up. The 1px band comes back at −0.4% mass — the ink total is essentially exact — with a peak of 49.8%, meaning its darkest pixel is 128 where the source was 0. The line did not lose ink. It spread it: one black row became two rows of mid-grey. Same energy, different object. That is exactly the failure the aggregate number cannot see, because spreading ink does not change how much ink there is. Lanczos piles on top of it: mass +19.2% with peak 44.3% — more total ink than the source had, in a stroke that is no longer black. That excess is the ringing the earlier table caught as a halo.

Below one pixel the limit is arithmetic, not filter choice. A 1px line downscaled 2× occupies half a pixel row; the darkest value it can possibly hold there is 127.5, whatever kernel you use. Sweep the width and the measurement tracks that floor closely:

Below 1px the measured value sits within a few points of the ceiling, which means no kernel has room to do better — the information left the file at the downscale. At 2px the ceiling is gone (such a stroke still occupies a full row when halved) and the measurement lands 26 points below it anyway, because a lone dark row cannot survive being upsampled by a smooth kernel. The 3px row is the one that misleads people: peak intact, mass +7.0% after one round trip. If you only tested thick lines you would conclude resampling is nearly free. It is not free — it is slower.

Repetition accumulates in the peak, not the mass. A file that passes through a thumbnail, a chat app and an editor gets the same down-and-up applied repeatedly. Mass settles instead of collapsing; the peak keeps falling:

A bug in this test that is worth copying. Building the source at exact coverage puts a 2px line at 128 / 0 / 128 — half-covered rows on either side. Read that in floating point and 127.5 < 128 is true, so all three rows count as ink and the band measures 15.00%. Read it after the round to uint8, which a real PNG forces, and 128 < 128 is false, so only the centre row counts and it measures 5.00%. Same image, a factor of three, decided by where the cast sits. And the uint8 reading for the 2px band is identical to the reading for the 1px band — at that point the metric cannot tell a 1px line from a 2px line. Every number in this section comes from the uint8 path, and the snippet below pins it there.

One consequence for the table at the top of this page: that measurement used the threshold method, so on its thin lines it was reporting a saturated zero as if it were a real loss. The direction it found is still right — smooth kernels do eat thin strokes — but the size of the effect it reported is a floor, not an estimate. Peak is the honest instrument, and it is one line of code:

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def peak_and_mass(img):
    a = np.asarray(img.convert("L"), np.uint8)        # uint8 first: the 127.5 boundary is real
    mass = (255.0 - a.astype(float)).sum() / 255.0    # ink, in full-black-pixel equivalents
    peak = (255.0 - a.min()) / 255.0 * 100            # how black the darkest pixel still is
    return round(peak, 1), round(mass, 1)

src = Image.open("drawing.png")
trip = src.resize((src.width // 2, src.height // 2), Image.BOX) \
          .resize(src.size, Image.LANCZOS)

print("source  ", peak_and_mass(src))
print("2x trip ", peak_and_mass(trip))

If the peak drops by more than a few points, the method did not resample the drawing, it diluted it — and ink coverage will never show you that, because the ink is still there.

What actually works on line art

The measurement points at a specific approach rather than a specific filter:

  1. Do not sharpen the whole image. Sharpening is the step that adds 20% excess ink. If you must sharpen, mask it to the lines and use a low amount — the overshoot is multiplicative, so 150% is far too much for binary content.
  2. Do not threshold at source resolution. If the source is a clean black-and-white drawing with no anti-aliasing, thresholding it is a no-op — the file is already binary. If it has any anti-aliasing, thresholding at 1× costs 2.0 dB against plain nearest and deletes 42% of 1px strokes depending only on where they landed on the pixel grid. Measured in the section below.
  3. For scans, deal with the paper first. A scanned drawing is line art plus paper texture plus JPEG noise. Upscaling amplifies all three. Cleaning before upscaling costs one step and removes two of the three problems.
  4. Check thin lines specifically. Do not evaluate on the thick contours — they survive everything. Look at the 1px details (eyelashes, hatching, thin text). That is where the 7% loss lands, and it is invisible in a thumbnail.

Why models do better here — and what they get wrong

A model trained on illustrations learns that a line is a line, not a gradient, and can reconstruct a crisp edge instead of averaging it. That is the genuine advantage over interpolation, and it is why anime-specific models exist.

The failure mode is equally specific: a model that has learned "illustration" will add line weight where it thinks line weight belongs. A sketch that was deliberately loose comes back with confident contours it never had. That is a style change disguised as an upscale, and on line art — where style is the content — it is a bigger change than any resolution difference.

The hairline that is not where you think it is

Everything above treats a line as a run of pixels. That is wrong in a way that matters more than filter choice, and it is easy to demonstrate. A 1px line in a drawing is not sitting on a pixel boundary. It is wherever the artist dragged the pen, which after export or scanning becomes a fractional offset: 30% of the ink in one pixel column and 70% in the next. The line is still 1px wide. It is just smeared across a phase you cannot see.

To measure what upscaling does to that, I generated 1px, 1.5px and 2px strokes at eight sub-pixel phases (0, 0.125, … 0.875), anti-aliased them with 8× area coverage the way a real export does, upscaled 3× with each filter, and measured two things per result: the full width at half maximum of the stroke (in source pixels) and its peak contrast as a percentage of black.

Three things fall out of that table, and the second one is the reason this section exists.

1. The 1px line comes out 38–46% thicker. Every filter reports a mean FWHM of 1.38–1.46px where the source measured 1.00px. The line did not grow because the image got bigger — it grew because half-maximum spreading is what a resampling kernel does to a feature narrower than its support. Lanczos is the worst offender here, which is the opposite of its reputation: it preserves sharpness on wide features and inflates narrow ones.

2. Phase, not filter, dominates the result. Look at FWHM σ. The nearest neighbour result — the crudest possible filter — varies by 0.48px across phases, which is nearly half a source pixel. Lanczos tightens that to 0.29px. So the choice between nearest and Lanczos is worth about 0.20px of thickness stability. The choice between "this stroke landed on offset 0.0" and "this stroke landed on offset 0.5" is worth a full 1.00px. A file does not contain strokes at one phase; it contains all of them, adjacent, so the output contains every thickness between 1px and 2px for lines that were drawn identically.

3. Contrast never recovers, it just stops dropping. Peak stroke darkness across the phase sweep spans 50% to 100% for a 1px source, and the upscale does not narrow that spread — it reproduces it. Below half-grey the stroke's own mid-tone pixels disappear from any binary count, which is why the ink-coverage test earlier in this article reads −12% for text rather than the full 50%: the strokes that lose the most ink are the ones already at the threshold, and they are lost silently.

Where the phase problem stops — measured, not guessed

The useful question is not "how do I fix hairlines" but "how thin does a line have to be before phase owns it". So I swept nominal width too, and measured the standard deviation of contrast across the eight phases. That number is the noise floor the file's own geometry imposes before any upscaler touches it:

Nominal widthSource σnearestbilinearbicubiclanczos
1.00px15.3pp15.315.314.212.9

The row at 2.00px is exactly zero for every method, because a 2px stroke always occupies at least one full pixel column at any phase — it cannot fall between the cracks. The row at 1.00px is 15.3pp, and there is nothing an interpolator can do about it, because nearest and bilinear both return the source value 15.3 unchanged. This is the whole argument in one table: upscaling cannot add phase information that the file does not contain.

Lanczos does genuinely help below 2px — 8.7pp down to 3.4pp at 1.5px, 4.0pp to 0.0pp at 1.75px. It averages neighbouring phases into a locally consistent thickness, which is why thin curves look smoother after a Lanczos upscale. But the price is in the first table: mean FWHM goes up from 1.38 to 1.46px. Lanczos buys stability by making the line fatter. On a drawing where line weight carries meaning, that trade deserves a conscious decision rather than a default.

Why this explains the −7% ink result from earlier. The ink-coverage test measured a 7% loss across a whole drawing. That 7% is an average over strokes of every width and every phase. Split by width and the average dissolves: 2px strokes lose nothing (σ = 0), 1px strokes lose up to half their contrast depending only on where they happened to be drawn. A single global number hides the fact that the damage is concentrated in the thinnest strokes at the worst phases — which is also why sharpening "recovers" 20%: it re-darkens the mid-tones that the filter diluted, at the cost of putting the same energy outside the line.

Reproducing the phase measurement

No image asset is needed; the strokes are generated. The trick that makes the test honest is supersampling the source at 8× and then integrating, so the source is anti-aliased the way a real export is, instead of being a clean binary strip that no real file contains:

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

SS = 8

def aastroke(n, w, offset, height=9):
    """horizontal stroke of width w starting at fractional x = offset"""
    x  = np.arange(n * SS) / SS
    x0, x1 = 40.0 + offset, 40.0 + offset + w
    cov = np.clip(np.minimum(x1, x + 1.0/SS) - np.maximum(x0, x), 0, None)
    row = 255.0 * (1.0 - cov.reshape(n, SS).sum(axis=1).clip(0, 1))
    return Image.fromarray(np.tile(row, (height, 1)).astype(np.uint8), "L")

def contrast(im):                     # peak darkness, 100 = fully black
    return (255.0 - np.asarray(im.convert("L"), float).min()) / 255.0 * 100

def fwhm(im, scale):                  # half-max width, back in source pixels
    row = 255.0 - np.asarray(im.convert("L"), float)[im.size[1] // 2]
    if row.max() < 1: return 0.0
    idx = np.where(row >= row.max() / 2)[0]
    return (idx[-1] - idx[0] + 1) / scale if len(idx) else 0.0

# sweep phase, not filter, first
for off in np.arange(0, 1.0, 0.125):
    src = aastroke(120, 1.0, off)
    up  = src.resize((360, 27), Image.LANCZOS)
    print(off, round(fwhm(up, 3), 2), round(contrast(up), 1))

If you run this and see the printed contrast fall from 100% to 60% and back as the offset sweeps 0 → 0.5 → 1.0, the test is working. That curve is the noise floor, and it is present in every line drawing you own.

What to do about it, in order

  1. Check stroke width before choosing a filter. If the drawing's thinnest meaningful strokes are below ~1.75px, phase is your dominant error term and no filter choice will outperform it. Above 2px, phase is zero for every method and filters behave the way the marketing says they do.
  2. Upscale less, not cleaner. The numbers above scale with the resize factor the same way the earlier ink test does: halving the scale factor keeps far more of the fine structure than any kernel change. A 1.5× upscale of a hairlined drawing loses almost nothing that a 4× upscale does not destroy outright.
  3. Treat thickening as a style change, then decide. Lanczos at 1.46px mean versus nearest at 1.38px is a 6% average weight increase — small on average, but the per-file effect is that every curve gets a little heavier while stair-stepped curves get smoother. If the drawing's line weight is the point, nearest-and-then-dealias is more faithful; if smoothness is the point, Lanczos is the better engineering choice.
  4. Do not sharpen a hairlined drawing. Sharpening attacks exactly the mid-tone fringe that phase created, and it does it selectively — the strokes at bad phases get over-darkened while the ones at good phases barely move. That is how a uniform set of lines becomes a visibly uneven set of lines after an "enhance".

The binary-structure advice, tested — and it fails on every file that has anti-aliasing

Two sections up, the advice list says: "Preserve binary structure if the image is binary. If the source is a clean black-and-white drawing (not a scan with paper texture), a threshold or vector approach beats any filter, because it never creates a mid-tone in the first place."

That sentence has been on this page since I wrote it, and it is the only recommendation in the list I had never measured. Every other item came out of a run. This one came out of reasoning about how thresholds work. So I tested it, and it is wrong — not in a footnote way, but by a larger margin than any filter choice discussed above. The short version: thresholding at source resolution costs 2.0 to 3.4 dB, and it deletes 42% of 1-pixel strokes depending only on where they sit on the pixel grid.

The reason it is worth a full section is that the mistake is invisible in the way people normally check. A thresholded line-art image looks crisp. It has no mid-tones at all, which is exactly the property the advice promises. It reads as clean in a thumbnail, it compresses smaller, and every metric that measures sharpness rather than accuracy improves. What it does not do is stay the drawing.

What was built, and why it had to be built this way

The scene has to be identical for every pipeline or the comparison is meaningless, so it is defined once in continuous coordinates and rendered by exact area coverage at 16× supersample, then box-integrated down. That produces three views of literally the same geometry:

  • the truth — the coverage map at 4× (what the drawing is at the scale we want),
  • the AA file — the same map at 1×, rounded to uint8, which is what a PNG export actually contains,
  • the binary file — the 1× map hard-thresholded, for the control case.

The scene is eight horizontal bars from 0.50 px to 3.00 px, each at a different sub-pixel offset, on a 96×96 frame. Every pipeline is scored against the same truth. No pipeline gets to be compared against its own output.

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

SS, W, H, OUT = 16, 96, 96, 4

def scene(scale):
    """exact area coverage, sampled at `scale` px per file px, box-integrated"""
    yy = np.arange(H * scale * SS) / (scale * SS) + .5 / (scale * SS)
    xx = np.arange(H * scale * SS) / (scale * SS) + .5 / (scale * SS)
    g = np.zeros((H * scale * SS, W * scale * SS))
    for i, w in enumerate([0.50, 0.60, 0.80, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50, 2.00, 3.00]):
        cy, dy = 8 + i * 10.0, (i * 0.37) % 1.0          # each bar at a different phase
        my = (yy >= cy + dy - w / 2) & (yy < cy + dy + w / 2)
        mx = (xx >= 6.0) & (xx < 70.0)
        g[np.ix_(my, mx)] = 1.0
    return g.reshape(H * scale, SS, W * scale, SS).mean(axis=(1, 3))

FILE  = np.round(255.0 - scene(1)   * 255).astype(np.uint8)   # the AA export
TRUTH = np.round(255.0 - scene(OUT) * 255).astype(np.uint8)   # ground truth at 4x
TH    = np.where(FILE < 128, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)          # the advice, applied

def psnr(x, y):
    mse = ((np.asarray(x, float) - np.asarray(y, float)) ** 2).mean()
    return 99.0 if mse == 0 else 10 * np.log10(255.0 ** 2 / mse)

fimg  = Image.fromarray(FILE, "L")
naive = Image.fromarray(TH, "L").resize((W * OUT, H * OUT), Image.NEAREST)
best  = fimg.resize((W * OUT, H * OUT), Image.LANCZOS)

print(round(psnr(best,  TRUTH), 2))   # 20.54  plain Lanczos, no threshold
print(round(psnr(naive, TRUTH), 2))   # 17.15  threshold then upscale

Result 1: "preserve binary structure" and "upscale an already-binary file" are the same operation

Before the table, the thing that makes it a trap rather than a preference. Thresholding an anti-aliased file and then upscaling it produces byte-identical output to upscaling a file that was binary to begin with. I checked all 147,456 pixels at 4×: 147,456 match, zero differ.

That collapses two of the options in the advice into one. "Preserve binary structure" is not a milder step than "threshold the source"; it is thresholding the source. Whatever a binary source costs you, this costs you the same. And a binary source, per the earlier ink test on this page, is the input that loses 12% of its ink to a smooth filter. So the advice is not "prefer binary content" — it is "start from the worst-case input on purpose".

Thresholding at source resolution is the worst row in the table. It costs 2.02 dB against nearest neighbour — the filter nobody recommends, the one with stair-stepping on every diagonal — and 3.39 dB against Lanczos. It delivers exactly the property it promises: the mid-tone column reads zero. That column is the problem, not the achievement. Every mid-tone that was carrying the sub-pixel position of a stroke has been rounded to one of two values, and the information in it is gone before the upscaler is ever called.

Note also that thresholding after the upscale is a different animal: bicubic at 4× then threshold reaches 20.43 dB, which beats plain nearest by 1.26 dB and misses plain bicubic by 0.13 dB. The ordering matters more than the threshold: moving the same threshold from before the resize to after it is worth 3.27 dB.

Result 3: the mechanism is a knife edge at exactly 127.5

The failure is not diffuse. It lands on one value, and the page already documented that value in the ink-mass section: a 1 px black line, box-downscaled 2×, becomes two rows of exactly 128, and 128 < 128 is false. Thresholding at 128 deletes it. Here is the same knife edge at 1×, with a stroke of nominal width 1.00 px centred exactly on a pixel centre:

>>> float min 127.50  ->  uint8 min 128  ->  DELETED
>>> # same 1px stroke centred on a pixel EDGE instead:
>>> uint8 min 0      ->  survives, as a full black row

One stroke, drawn at the same weight by the same artist, survives as solid black or vanishes completely depending on a half-pixel shift of where it happened to land when the canvas was exported. The threshold is not selecting thin strokes for removal. It is selecting strokes whose coverage rounds to 128 for removal, which is a different and much less defensible set.

This is the same class of error as the uint8/float boundary described earlier on this page — the 15.00% versus 5.00% measurement that differed by a factor of three because of where the cast sat. It is worth saying plainly: that was not an isolated curiosity about a measurement, it is a property of the file format, and it bites the pipeline too.

Result 4: it deletes 42% of 1-pixel strokes, and recall is a 2-D function

The page's earlier phase sweep varied only the horizontal phase of horizontal bars, which is a 1-D sweep. A threshold is a 2-D decision — a contour, a hatch mark, a square screentone dot all have edges on both axes, and a bad phase on either axis is enough to kill the mark. So I repeated the sweep as a square mark over 8×8 = 64 sub-pixel placements:

At 1.00 px, 27 of 64 placements are deleted. That is 42%. The 1-D sweep on the same width reported 7 of 8 surviving — 87.5% — because a horizontal bar only has a phase along one axis. The 2-D answer is about two thirds of the 1-D answer. The earlier section on this page understates the damage for exactly the features it was warning about, and I would rather correct that here than leave a method that flatters its own conclusion.

Two things follow from the rightmost column. Recall is not survival: at 1.25 px all but five placements survive, but their peak contrast spans 55% to 100%, so a field of identical dots becomes a field of dots at visibly different densities. And at 0.75 px the nine survivors all sit at exactly 56% — the threshold either deletes the mark or leaves a ghost at half strength, with nothing in between. There is no threshold value that fixes this, because the choice being made is binary and the input is not.

Result 5: where the advice is right, and why it is a tautology there

One case behaves as advertised. Take a source that is genuinely black-and-white — every pixel 0 or 255, no anti-aliasing at all — and thresholding it changes zero pixels. Nearest-neighbour upscale and threshold-then-upscale are byte-identical. The advice is correct.

But look at what it says in that case. A file with no anti-aliasing has nothing for a threshold to do. "Preserve binary structure if the image is binary" reduces to "if the file is already thresholded, it is already thresholded." It is not a technique that beats filters; it is a no-op described as an advantage. The only way the sentence can mean something is if you apply it to a file that is not binary — and in that case, per the table above, it costs 2 to 3.4 dB.

Whether such a source exists in practice is the open question, not the recommendation. A drawing exported from a vector tool with anti-aliasing enabled is not binary. One exported with it disabled is — and then the advice tells you to do nothing, which is also the right answer, but for a reason that has nothing to do with thresholds.

What to do instead

  1. Threshold after the resize, never before. Moving the same threshold to after the upscale is worth 3.27 dB. If you want a two-value output, take it at the target resolution.
  2. If you are thresholding, threshold high, not at 128. The knife edge sits at 127.5 because that is where a half-covered pixel rounds to. A level of 160 takes 1 px stroke recall from 37/64 to 59/64, at the cost of fattening a 1.25 px stroke by 62% (1.17 → 1.89 pixels of area). Neither level is right; know which error you are choosing.
  3. Check a 1 px feature at four phases before you commit. Four placements is enough to see the spread. If your mark survives at 0.0 and dies at 0.5, the pipeline has a phase problem and no filter setting will fix it.
  4. Do not use a threshold to make a drawing look sharp. It will. That is the measurement: mid-tone pixels go to zero and the picture reads crisper while being further from the truth than the blurry version. It is the same trap as sharpening, one step harder to undo.

What this measurement does not cover. The scene is synthetic and has no paper texture, no JPEG ringing, no colour. It establishes the direction and rough size of the effect on clean geometric strokes, which is the case the advice names. It does not establish that a real scanned drawing loses exactly 42% of its hairlines — scans carry their own noise that can push a marginal stroke either way across the threshold. If you have a real file and a real pen, the four-phase check in item 3 is the part worth running.

Reproducing it

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image, ImageFilter

gt = Image.open("drawing.png")          # the source you are testing

# ink coverage = fraction of pixels below mid-grey
ink = (np.asarray(gt.convert("L"), dtype=float) < 128).mean() * 100

# the four-step test
small = gt.resize((gt.width // 2, gt.height // 2), Image.LANCZOS)   # 2x down
back  = small.resize(gt.size, Image.BICUBIC)                        # 2x up
sharp = back.filter(ImageFilter.UnsharpMask(radius=1.0, percent=150, threshold=0))

Screentone is the other half of the frame

Everything above is about strokes. A cel frame also carries screentone, and that behaves by different numbers: a 6 px screen survives a 4x upscale at 95-100% of its measured dot depth, while a 2 px screen arrives at the resampler with only 9% of its depth left and gets the rest invented. I measured both, plus where the 1.26 levels of brightness drift comes from, in the screentone measurement.

Try it on your own drawing

The ink-coverage test takes seconds and tells you more than a side-by-side: threshold your result at mid-grey and compare the coverage to the source. If it moved more than a few percent, the method changed your line weight, and no amount of resolution fixes that.
