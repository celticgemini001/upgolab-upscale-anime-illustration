Upscaling Line Art Without Destroying the Lines

At 4×, interpolated upscaling loses 12% of the ink pixels in a line drawing. Then sharpening adds 20% back. The lost 12% is softness; the added 20% is the halo that makes a clean drawing look like it is glowing.

14 November 2026 · ink-coverage measurement on a synthetic line-art test

Line art breaks upscalers in a way photographs do not, and the reason is that line art has almost no mid-tones. A photo is a continuous field of values where an average is meaningful. A drawing is a binary decision — ink or paper — and every resampling filter is built to produce averages, which on a binary image means a grey fringe that belongs to neither.

The measurement

Test image: horizontal lines at 1, 2 and 3px width plus a 2px circle outline, black on white, 400×400. Downscale to 200×200 (2×), upscale back to 400×400 with each method, then measure ink coverage — the percentage of pixels below mid-grey. Ground truth is 1.851%.

MethodInk coverageDelta vs truth
nearest1.978%+0.127
nearest + unsharp2.238%+0.387
bicubic1.722%−0.129
bicubic + unsharp2.216%+0.365
lanczos1.711%−0.140
lanczos + unsharp2.180%+0.329

Three things to read out of this:

1. The smooth filters eat lines. Bicubic and Lanczos both land around 1.71–1.72%, about 7% below the ground truth. A 1px line upscaled with a good interpolation kernel becomes a 1px line with two grey shoulders — and at the threshold where you count "ink," those shoulders fall on the wrong side. Thin lines get thinner, then disappear.

2. Nearest over-preserves. It lands at 1.978%, above the truth, because it replicates hard pixel blocks. That is not accuracy — it is a blocky line that is nominally the right thickness but visually wrong, with stair-stepping along every diagonal. If you have ever upscaled line art with nearest and wondered why the curves look like a staircase, this is the number behind it.

3. Sharpening overshoots hard. Every sharpened variant jumps to 2.18–2.24%, which is 18–21% above the ground truth. Unsharp mask works by subtracting a blurred copy, and on a line that produces a dark core with bright rails on either side. Those bright rails, and the enlarged dark core, both count as ink. The line is now thicker than it was drawn, with a halo.

Why this is worse for line art than for photos. On a photo, a halo lands on texture that partially masks it. On line art, the halo lands on flat white paper. There is nothing to hide it, so a sharpened drawing reads as slightly luminous — a characteristic "AI upscale" look that people recognise without being able to name.

What actually works on line art

The measurement points at a specific approach rather than a specific filter:

  1. Do not sharpen the whole image. Sharpening is the step that adds 20% excess ink. If you must sharpen, mask it to the lines and use a low amount — the overshoot is multiplicative, so 150% is far too much for binary content.
  2. Preserve binary structure if the image is binary. If the source is a clean black-and-white drawing (not a scan with paper texture), a threshold or vector approach beats any filter, because it never creates a mid-tone in the first place.
  3. For scans, deal with the paper first. A scanned drawing is line art plus paper texture plus JPEG noise. Upscaling amplifies all three. Cleaning before upscaling costs one step and removes two of the three problems.
  4. Check thin lines specifically. Do not evaluate on the thick contours — they survive everything. Look at the 1px details (eyelashes, hatching, thin text). That is where the 7% loss lands, and it is invisible in a thumbnail.

Why models do better here — and what they get wrong

A model trained on illustrations learns that a line is a line, not a gradient, and can reconstruct a crisp edge instead of averaging it. That is the genuine advantage over interpolation, and it is why anime-specific models exist.

The failure mode is equally specific: a model that has learned "illustration" will add line weight where it thinks line weight belongs. A sketch that was deliberately loose comes back with confident contours it never had. That is a style change disguised as an upscale, and on line art — where style is the content — it is a bigger change than any resolution difference.

Reproducing it

# ink coverage = fraction of pixels below mid-grey
ink = (np.asarray(img.convert("L"), dtype=float) < 128).mean() * 100

# the four-step test
small = gt.resize((200, 200), Image.LANCZOS)      # 2x down
back  = small.resize((400, 400), Image.BICUBIC)   # 2x up
sharp = back.filter(ImageFilter.UnsharpMask(radius=1.0, percent=150, threshold=0))

Try it on your own drawing

The ink-coverage test takes seconds and tells you more than a side-by-side: threshold your result at mid-grey and compare the coverage to the source. If it moved more than a few percent, the method changed your line weight, and no amount of resolution fixes that.
